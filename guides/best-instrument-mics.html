Best Microphones for Recording Every Instrument

No single microphone covers everything, and after twenty years on stages and in studios I can tell you the pairing that actually gets used on each instrument — plus the two affordable mics worth buying before anything else.

By Daniel Carnago · August 2026

Touring and session musician with 20+ years on stages around the world. A great deal of the gear I recommend here I have used myself, on tours, in professional studios, or in my own studio.

How I test gear: to be honest, I have not tested every product on this site. Much of it I have used myself in live shows and recording sessions; others I have seen tested by musicians and sound techs I trust; and the rest I have researched in depth before recommending.
Verdict
Buy the SM57 and RE20 first, then add one or two specialist mics for the instrument you record most.

Electric Guitar & Amp Miking: The Royer R-121 + SM57 Combo

For the classic rock midrange, put a Shure SM57 dead on the speaker cone. When you want a bigger, wider amp sound, blend in a Royer R-121 pulled back a few inches. The SM57 gives you that 5-7kHz presence bite that fights its way through any mix, and the R-121 rounds things out with an open top and a rich low end that makes a cab sound expensive. Together they're the most-used two-mic rig in recorded history. Tracking heavy rhythm? Drop an e906 or M 201 in there for extra thickness.

Bass Guitar: RE20, D6, and the low-end specialists

For bass, grab an Audix D6 for a modern, punchy bottom with real attack, or an Electro-Voice RE20 for a fatter, rounder broadcast-style sound. The D6 is built for low-frequency sources — that 60Hz bump translates instantly on any speaker. The RE20 is the old-school choice for bass cab and DI: its variable-D design pushes away proximity boom so the low end stays clean. Both sit nicely next to a DI feed for a full hybrid bass tone.

Acoustic Guitar: The KM 184 and OC818 Clean Condensers

For shimmering, transparent acoustic guitar, a Neumann KM 184 or an Austrian Audio OC818 is the way to go. The small-diaphragm KM 184 catches the sparkle and note definition of steel and nylon strings in fine detail. The OC818 is a bigger, more flexible condenser: switchable polar patterns and dual outputs let you shape the EQ response later — even from the PolarPilot app. If you need fast, natural results, the KM 184 is the safe pro pick.

Horns & Brass: The Royer R-121 and MD 421 classics

Trumpets and sax sound best through a ribbon — a Royer R-121 smooths out that piercing 2-4kHz bite without losing body. Close up on a horn section, the MD 421 brings weight and presence without harshness. And a Coles 4038 on sax and cello gives you that round, vintage character you can't fake with a dynamic.

Strings & Orchestra: The DPA 4099 CORE and Coles 4038

For violin and cello, clip on a DPA 4099 CORE — its supercardioid pattern and CORE preamp capture honest, high-resolution tone with zero feedback on stage. In the studio, the Coles 4038 is a dream on string sections; its warm, focused character sits beautifully in front of the ensemble. For acoustic orchestras, a pair of Coles as overheads is the classic pro move.

Kick-ass Drum Kits: Overheads, Toms and Kick

A classic drum setup combines a kick mic, snare mic, tom mics and overheads: kick with an Audix D6 or Shure Beta 52A, snare with an SM57, toms with a three-pack of Sennheiser e604s, and overheads with Neumann KM 184s. The D6 and Beta 52A both give a modern, punchy kick; the SM57 grabs that snare crack; the compact e604 clips straight onto the toms and stays put; and the transparent KM 184s carry the whole kit in stereo up top. You end up with a full, controllable drum sound that sits right in the mix.

Hand Percussion: Congas, Bongos and Cajón

The same mics you use on a drum kit work on congas and bongos — you don't need a separate percussion setup. Conga and bongo skins ring with fast transients, so a dynamic with a presence peak like the Shure SM57 or Sennheiser e906 picks up both the slap and the pitch clearly. Want more body and warmth on a conga solo? The Sennheiser MD 421 is a classic — its low-mid response catches the drum's deep tone without smothering the slap. On a cajón, put an SM57 or a condenser a few inches off the face for that mix of punch and wood. And if you're recording a whole percussion section in stereo, hang a pair of KM 184s or a Coles as overhead — the congas, bongos, shakers and cowbells turn into one instrument instead of a bunch of spot mics.

Conclusion

There's no single 'best microphone' — there's the right mic for each instrument. Keep an SM57 and an RE20 around as your go-to pair, add an R-121 and a pair of Neumanns for guitars and acoustics, an e604 pack for drums and a DPA 4099 for strings, and you can record almost anything to a professional standard.
