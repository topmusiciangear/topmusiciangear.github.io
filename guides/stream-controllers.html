Stream Deck Alternatives & Best Audio Mixers for Creators

Streaming sound is 90% routing, and the gear below turns that routing into something you can grab with your hands. The Stream Deck+, the GoXLR Mini, the RODECaster Pro II, the Yamaha AG08, the Mackie MainStream, the Streamer X, the DLZ Creator and the Wave XLR are the top streaming controllers and mixers — here is what each one does best.

By Daniel Carnago · October 2026

Touring and session musician with 20+ years on stages around the world. A great deal of the gear I recommend here I have used myself, on tours, in professional studios, or in my own studio.

Verdict
The Stream Deck+ is the total control pick with buttons, dials and a touch strip for every stream setup: plus the XLR Dock turns it into a full interface. The GoXLR Mini is the streamer-first mixer with real faders and a Midas preamp, the Yamaha AG08 is the analog-feel pick with a fader per app, the RODECaster Pro II is the broadcast-console pick that survives a frozen PC, and the Mackie MainStream is the one-cable console pick with HDMI capture. How to Choose a Streaming Mixer and Controller

A streaming mixer is the physical brain of your stream audio. It keeps your mic, your game, your music and your chat on separate channels so viewers hear a clean mix instead of one giant muddled blob. Most people start with cheap software routing and buy a real unit the first time something goes wrong mid-stream.

First, pick the shape. Do you want a controller that drives your existing software, a dedicated mixer that processes audio, or an all-in-one box? The Elgato Stream Deck+ is the best-selling controller, period — it sits next to whatever you already own and gives you buttons and dials for everything. The TC Helicon GoXLR Mini is the streamer-first mixer with real faders, a Midas preamp and per-app routing. The Rode RODECaster Pro II runs the whole broadcast on hardware for streamers and podcasters. The Mackie MainStream merges interface and HDMI capture in one USB-C cable. The Streamer X throws an interface and a 4K capture card into the same compact box. And the Wave XLR is just a really good mic interface that plugs into the Elgato world.

Now check the software, because that is what you will live with. All of these run through desktop apps, and that app shapes your whole workflow. The one you enjoy using every single day is the one you will keep on your desk. Note the OS limits: GoXLR Mini and MainStream Matrix are Windows only, while Rode Central and Roland software cover both systems.

Is the Elgato Stream Deck+ the Best Streaming Controller?

The Elgato Stream Deck+ is the controller that keeps selling out for a reason. Right now it sits as the #1 Best Seller in digital audio workstation controllers, and once you spend an afternoon with it, the why is obvious. Eight LCD keys, four haptic dials and a thin touch strip sit under your fingers, and each one can trigger anything — switching scenes, toggling your mic, dimming your lights, jumping between Spotify playlists.

Where it shines is the plugins. The Wave Link plugin alone turns the deck into a full audio surface: mute channels, flip effects, swap outputs, all without touching the mouse. OBS, Twitch, Discord, smart home — there are many plugins, and multi-action support fires a whole chain with one press. Set up a smart profile and it even reconfigures itself when you tab between apps.

What it will never do: mix audio. It has no XLR input and no preamp. Think of it as the conductor's baton, not the orchestra — pair it with a Wave XLR or any interface and you have a complete desk.

Pro tip: the official Elgato XLR Dock bolts underneath and turns the Stream Deck+ into a complete XLR interface (80 dB gain, onboard effects, Wave Link integration) — controller and mic preamp in one footprint, no separate interface needed.

Is the Rode Streamer X worth it? (Software Explained Plainly)

The Rode Streamer X is the most clever space-saver on this list. It squeezes audio interface, a 4K capture card and a little control surface into one compact box, so console streamers and dual-PC folks can ditch three separate pieces of gear. The Revolution preamp pushes 76dB of gain, which is a lot — a Shure SM7B or an EV RE20 sounds full and loud with zero boosters in the chain. Video side, you get 4K30 capture with 4K60 passthrough and VRR support, plus four SMART pads for sounds and voice FX.

Now the part to be clear about: the software. The hardware is great; the UNIFY app is where the complaints live, and they are fair. It can be unstable upon reboots and requires precise power-cycling — wake your PC from sleep and the virtual mixer sometimes loses audio route, leaving your mic silent until you close and reopen the app. That matches what user reviews report after some digging.

Who should buy it? If you stream from a console or a single PC and want one tidy box that handles audio and video, it is hard to beat for the money. If your setup is a complex dual-PC machine and you need software that never flinches, the Elgato ecosystem is the safer bet.

Is the Elgato Wave XLR MK.2 the Best Mic Interface for Streamers?

The Elgato Wave XLR MK.2 is the quiet dependable pick in the Elgato family. It takes any XLR microphone and makes it sound broadcast-ready. The headline number is 80dB of ultra-low-noise gain, that is enough clean power to drive an SM7B without any booster sitting between the mic and the box. You also get 24-bit/96kHz conversion, tap-to-mute, and a dial that flips between gain, voice tune, headphone volume and the mic/PC mix.

What makes the MK.2 special is the Wave FX Processor. Built with LEWITT Audio, it runs five onboard DSP effects — low-cut, expander, voice tune, compressor and EQ — plus VST plugins, all on the hardware with zero-latency monitoring. Your processed voice sounds the same in every app because the processing follows the mic itself, not the software. No virtual cables, no fiddly routing.

Clipguard 2.0 is a small blessing. With 135dB of dynamic range, loud peaks get caught before they ever distort, so you can stop babysitting your gain and just talk. The honest limitation is that it is an interface, not a controller — you still want a Stream Deck alongside it to run your stream.

Behringer FLOW 8: What Six Faders and Bluetooth Control Actually Buy You

The FLOW 8 is the one in this list that behaves like a studio mixer instead of a creator gadget, and it costs less than half what a RØDECaster does. It is not an audio interface with some buttons bolted on. It is a genuine eight-channel digital mixer: two XLR mic inputs, two combo XLR/TRS inputs, two pairs of balanced stereo line inputs with a Hi-Z jack for guitar or bass, and balanced XLR main outputs with monitor sends on 1/4" TRS. Six 60mm channel faders ride on top, with the two on the far right each combining a stereo pair, so channels 5/6 and 7/8 share one fader.

What sets it apart from the other boxes here is where the processing lives. Every channel gets four-band EQ and a compressor, and the monitor and main buses get a nine-band EQ with a limiter on the output. The maths runs in 32-bit floating point, which is the same internal precision the RØDECaster Pro II uses. Two independent effects engines sit on top, each with 16 presets, plus 12 reverb programs.

You drive all of it from the free FLOW app. The app talks to the mixer over Bluetooth, and Behringer is explicit that it does not need a Wi-Fi network, which means you can adjust EQ, recall FX and change routing from your phone during a stream. That same Bluetooth chip also does A2DP, so your phone can stream audio into the mixer as another source. Over USB it is a 10-in/2-out interface at 48 kHz and 24-bit, recording up to 10 tracks.

Two things to know before you buy. The faders are not motorized, so on a show the app and the physical fader can drift apart and the offset LEDs are there to tell you when they have. And EQ, effects and routing are only reachable in the app, not from the surface, so this is not the box for someone who wants to tweak everything with their hands mid-show.
