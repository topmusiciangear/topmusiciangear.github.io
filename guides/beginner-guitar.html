Yamaha FG800 for acoustic songwriting, Yamaha Pacifica 112V for electric beginners, Squier Affinity Stratocaster for genuine Fender feel, Epiphone Les Paul Special-I for budget rock, and the Yamaha C40 if you want the gentlest nylon start. All under $450 and proven to keep beginners playing.

Pros
  • Spruce top with scalloped X-bracing projects a balanced tone that fills a room
  • Easy-to-play neck profile is ideal for learning chords and transitions
  • Gloss body and chrome tuners give a premium look
Cons
  • Laminate back and sides trade resonance for durability
  • Full-size dreadnought is still a big body for smaller players
  • No electronics for plugging in or recording direct
Squier Affinity Series Stratocaster
Pros
  • Classic Strat shape, comfortable C-neck and genuine single-coil sparkle at budget prices
  • Light enough for younger and smaller players to manage easily
  • Sealed die-cast tuners and dependable hardware keep it stable for years
  • A genuine step up in feel from the very cheapest electrics
Cons
  • Stock ceramic pickups sound decent but lack the depth of pricier alnico units
  • Cheapest trim level skips some premium hardware touches
  • You will likely still want a pro setup for the lowest possible action
Epiphone Les Paul Special-I
Pros
  • Pair of punchy humbuckers delivers thick, rock-ready tone on a budget
  • Slim, light single-cut body is comfortable to hold for long practice sessions
  • Simple volume/tone/switch wiring is easy to learn on
  • Looks and plays the part of a proper Les Paul for a fraction of the cost
Cons
  • No tremolo — vibrato comes from the fingers alone
  • Two humbuckers are less versatile than an HSS configuration
  • Budget hardware may benefit from an upgrade down the line
Yamaha C40 Classical (Nylon) Guitar
Pros
  • Nylon strings are far gentler on fingertips than steel — the easiest way to start
  • Warm, balanced tone from spruce over meranti for classical and fingerstyle
  • Proven reliability and consistent quality make it the classic student pick
Cons
  • Limited to classical, flamenco and fingerstyle tones
  • Wider classical neck takes getting used to if you're switching from steel-string
  • No electronics for amplification

Acoustic vs Electric: Which First?

The honest answer: whichever one you'll play. People will tell you to start on acoustic because "it builds finger strength." That's true, but it also builds frustration if you want to play rock and you're stuck on a guitar with heavy strings and high action. If your heroes play electric, get electric. Motivation beats technique every time.

Acoustic guitars are simpler — no amp needed, grab and play. The Yamaha FG800 and Fender CD-60S are the two budget dreadnoughts beginners keep coming back to. Electric guitars need an amplifier but have lighter strings and lower action, which is easier on beginner fingers — the Yamaha Pacifica 112V, Squier Affinity Stratocaster and Epiphone Les Paul Special-I all keep you comfortably in that range.

Classical guitars with nylon strings are the gentlest on fingertips — the Yamaha C40 is the classic choice — but they're limited to classical and flamenco styles. There's no wrong answer, just the answer you'll actually play.

What to Look For in a First Guitar

Action, tuning stability, and comfort matter more than brand. Action is the distance between the strings and the fretboard — high action means sore fingers and notes that are hard to press down. Most guitars in the $150+ range have decent action out of the box, but cheaper models often need a setup ($40–$60 at a local shop) to play properly.

Tuning stability means the guitar stays in tune after you've been playing for a while. Cheap tuning machines slip, which means you're retuning between every song. Look for guitars with sealed tuners (they have a closed back, unlike the open-gear type on very cheap models) — the Pacifica 112V, Affinity Stratocaster and Squier Sonic Mustang all hold up well here.

Comfort is personal — pick up the guitar and see if the neck feels right in your hand. Some necks are thick (vintage Fender), some are thin (Ibanez, and the short-scale Squier Sonic Mustang), and some are somewhere in between. There's no "best" shape — only what feels right for you Player Strat vs Pacifica

Songwriting Guitars

For songwriting, you want a guitar that sounds good unplugged and inspires you to play. Acoustic guitars are the natural choice here — no setup needed, grab it from the corner and start writing. The Yamaha FG800 ($229) is the standard recommendation for beginners who want to write songs. It's not exciting, but it's reliable, stays in tune, and sounds decent.

On a tighter budget, the Squier Sonic Mustang is a short-scale electric that's light, easy to reach across, and full of classic single-coil character — great for strumming out ideas quietly at home or through a small amp. The Fender CD-60S also holds its own as a warm, mellow writing companion.
