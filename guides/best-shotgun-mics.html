The 9 Best Shotgun Mics for Film & Video (2026)

A great shotgun mic is the difference between amateur hiss and broadcast-ready dialogue. These are the nine boom and camera-mount shotguns that consistently earn their keep - from the starter to the industry-standard MKH 416.

By Daniel Carnago · August 2026

Touring and session musician with 20+ years on stages around the world. A great deal of the gear I recommend here I have used myself, on tours, in professional studios, or in my own studio.

How I test gear: to be honest, I have not tested every product on this site. Much of it I have used myself in live shows and recording sessions; others I have seen tested by musicians and sound techs I trust; and the rest I have researched in depth before recommending.
Verdict
The MKH 416 wins on proven reliability and resale value; the NTG5 wins on weight and noise floor; the MKE 600 wins for everyone else.
Sennheiser MKH 416
Pros
  • RF-condenser design is virtually immune to humidity and temperature changes
  • Industry-standard supercardioid reach that flatters dialogue with minimal EQ
  • Legendary durability - decades of rental-house abuse are normal for this mic
  • Holds resale value better than almost any microphone
Cons
  • Requires 48V phantom power - no battery option
  • Only the specialist MKH 50 costs more in this guide
  • Metal body is heavier than modern lightweight shotguns
  • Included clip is basic; you will want a quality suspension
Rode NTG5
Pros
  • Quietest mic here at 10 dBA - dialogue stays clean in quiet scenes
  • At 76 g, long boom takes are effortless
  • RF-bias circuit shrugs off humidity like the 416
  • Complete kit: WS10 windshield, cable, mount and hard case
Cons
  • 120 dB SPL ceiling limits headroom on loud sets
  • 3.5mm output is less road-proof than locking XLR
  • Costs more than the MKE 600 for similar reach
  • Needs phantom or USB-C power - no battery option
Sennheiser MKE 600
Pros
  • Supercardioid reach and switchable low-cut at a third of the 416's price
  • Runs on one AA or 48V phantom - fits any rig
  • 132 dB SPL handles loud scenes without a pad
  • Real metal body and XLR connection
Cons
  • 15 dBA self-noise is audible in very quiet rooms
  • Battery/phantom switch is easy to leave in the wrong position
  • No worthwhile suspension or windshield included
  • Rear lobe picks up more room than RF designs
Audio-Technica AT875R
Pros

  • Integral XLRM connector - no cable joint to fail at the mic end
  • Disciplined line-plus-gradient pattern with clean side rejection
  • Runs on any phantom supply from 11-52 V, so almost every recorder feeds it
Cons
  • No battery option - you need phantom power everywhere you shoot
  • About 20 dBA self-noise asks for clean preamps in quiet scenes
  • Top end less refined than pricier tubes in this guide
  • Fixed design: no pad or low-cut switches on the mic
Deity S-Mic 3
Pros
  • Measured 12 dBA self-noise undercuts the 416 on paper
  • 80 g - the lightest full-size boom here
  • Weather-resistant aluminum body tested to 95% humidity
  • Neutral tone that sits dialogue forward without coloration

  • P24/P48 only - no battery option

How to Choose the Best Shotgun Microphone?

Shotgun mics use interference tubes to reject sound from the sides, which is exactly what you want when dialogue must float above traffic, crowds or room reflections. Three specs matter most. Self-noise below 16 dBA keeps quiet scenes clean. An RF-bias or waterproof design survives humid locations that destroy ordinary condensers. And a supercardioid lobe gives you forgiving placement on the boom without losing isolation. On-camera models trade some reach for convenience - they sit closer to the lens than to your subject - while full-size booms reward you with cinema-grade separation. Match the mic to how you actually shoot, and any of the picks below will outperform your camera's built-in audio by a mile.

Best Overall: Sennheiser MKH 416

The MKH 416 is the shotgun you hear on almost every TV set and news stand-up. Its RF-condenser capsule is famously immune to humidity and temperature swings, which is why location crews trust it from desert exteriors to tropical rain. The supercardioid pattern reaches cleanly across a boom-length gap, and the flat, present response needs almost no EQ to sit in a mix. it costs more than most beginner kits entirely - but it also holds resale value like few mics in this business. If you shoot for clients, this is the last shotgun you may ever need to buy.

Best Modern Alternative: Rode NTG5

The NTG5 brings shotgun design into the 2020s. At just 76 grams it is the lightest full-size boom in this guide and disappears on a pole during long takes, while its RF-bias circuit measures a class-leading 10 dBA of self-noise - quieter than mics costing twice as much. It powers from P48/P24 phantom or straight off a USB-C power bank, and the kit ships genuinely complete: WS10 windshield, high-grade cable, mount and hard case. Tonally it reads slightly warmer than the 416 rather than brighter, which flatters naturalistic documentary dialogue. The trade-offs: a 120 dB SPL ceiling gives less headroom for shouting actors, and the 3.5mm jack feels less road-proof than a locking XLR.

Best Value Boom: Sennheiser MKE 600

No shotgun has launched more indie careers than the MKE 600. you get genuine supercardioid reach, a switchable low-cut for handling rumble, and the rare flexibility of running on one AA battery or 48V phantom - so it pairs with anything from a Zoom recorder to a cinema camera. Its self-noise is higher than the NTG5's, and the plastic body won't survive the abuse a 416 shrugs off, but for narrative shorts, weddings and YouTube productions it remains the benchmark every budget mic is measured against.

Best Ultra-Compact Boom: Audio-Technica AT875R

The AT875R is the shortest way to real boom rejection. At just 175 mm and 80 grams it slots into cramped interiors, handheld rigs and travel kits where full broadcast tubes will not fit, yet it keeps a disciplined line-plus-gradient pattern with clean side rejection. The XLRM connector is integral, so there is no cable joint to rattle at the mic end. It runs on any phantom supply from 11-52 V - but there is no battery option, so plan your power. Its roughly 20 dBA self-noise wants decent preamps and the top end is less refined than pricier tubes here. Still, it turns any recorder into a legitimate dialogue rig.

Best Budget Pro: Deity S-Mic 3

Deity built the S-Mic 3 to embarrass mics twice its price. An all-aluminum weather-resistant body (tested to 95% humidity), just 80 grams and a measured 12 dBA self-noise - quieter than the 416 on paper - make it sound far more expensive than. The tonal balance is neutral and honest, sitting dialogue forward without coloration. Timecode-synced filmmakers already trust Deity wireless; this boom completes the ecosystem. The trade-offs are practical, not sonic: availability outside direct channels is spottier than Sennheiser or Rode, the company's track record is shorter, and there is no battery option - it needs P24 or P48 phantom.

Best On-Camera: Rode VideoMic NTG

The VideoMic NTG collapses the boom bag into the hot shoe. It borrows the NTG-series capsule family, adds infinitely variable gain, a -20 dB pad, safety channel, 75/150 Hz filters and even doubles as a USB-C audio interface for voiceovers. Auto power-on wakes it with the camera, and the internal battery charges over USB-C. It cannot match a proper boom for reach, but for run-and-gun docs, vlogs and B-roll it replaces an entire audio chain with one rechargeable unit.

The MKE 400 is the painless first step beyond your camera's built-in mic. Best for Indoor Dialogue: Sennheiser MKH 50

The MKH 50 is what sound mixers reach for when walls get close. It shares the 416's RF-condenser humidity immunity and its 12 dBA self-noise, but trades the interference tube for a pure supercardioid - no rear lobe grabbing wall reflections, no comb-filtering in reverberant rooms. That is why it is the standard indoor dialogue mic on scripted sets, paired with the 416 outdoors. A switchable -10 dB pad and low-cut tame hot rooms, and it ships genuinely complete with the MZS 40 suspension and MZW 41 windshield. it is a specialist's tool - but if you shoot mostly interiors, nothing here flatters dialogue like it.

Conclusion

For dedicated boom work the Sennheiser MKH 416 remains the professional default, with the Rode NTG5 as its lighter modern rival and the MKH 50 ruling reflective interiors. Indie budgets should buy the MKE 600 without hesitation. For camera-top duty, the VideoMic NTG is unmatched, and the MKE 400 is the easiest first upgrade.
