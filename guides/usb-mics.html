Best USB & XLR Microphones for Streaming

A great stream mic is a great stream mic whether it plugs into USB or XLR — what matters is that it fits your room and your workflow. Here are the seven mics I actually recommend for streaming and podcasting in 2026.

By Daniel Carnago · August 2026

Touring and session musician with 20+ years on stages around the world. A great deal of the gear I recommend here I have used myself, on tours, in professional studios, or in my own studio.

How I test gear: to be honest, I have not tested every product on this site. Much of it I have used myself in live shows and recording sessions; others I have seen tested by musicians and sound techs I trust; and the rest I have researched in depth before recommending.
Verdict
There's no single best USB mic — the right one fits your room and how you work. Pick a dynamic for noisy spaces, a condenser for detail, and match it to your setup.
Shure SM7B
Pros
  • The default streamer microphone — the warm, intimate sound of every major podcast
  • Dynamic cardioid rejects room noise, keyboard clicks, and PC fan whine
  • Rolled-off highs tame sibilance so 'S' and 'T' sounds stay smooth, even when you lean into the mic
  • Built-in shock mount kills mechanical noise from your desk
Cons
  • Quiet mic — needs a clean interface or a Cloudlifter to stay noise-free
  • $399 plus the cost of extra gain gear
  • XLR only — no single-cable USB option
  • No headphone output or built-in DSP — the MV7+ adds zero-latency monitoring and auto-level in the mic
  • At $399 it costs $130 more than the NT1, which adds 32-bit float and USB-C recording for less
Electro-Voice RE20
Pros
  • The radio broadcast standard — a flat, authoritative voice
  • Variable-D design kills the proximity effect — consistent low end from 2 to 6 inches
  • Smooth, natural response with a subtle presence lift — needs far less EQ than most dynamics
  • Equally legendary on kick drum, guitar cabs, and horns
Cons
  • XLR only, heavier than the SM7B, and wants a clean high-gain preamp or Cloudlifter
  • $449 — $50 more than the SM7B and the most expensive option here
  • Big and heavy on a desk or arm compared to the MV7+ or Wave:3
  • No built-in shock mount or pop filter — the MV7+ and NT1 ship with both
  • Its 45 Hz–18 kHz response is narrower than the NT1's 20 Hz–20 kHz at both extremes
Shure MV7+
Pros
  • The same warm SM7B family tone over USB-C, XLR, or both with built-in DSP
  • Headphone output with zero-latency monitoring
  • Real-time auto level mode rides your gain while you talk
  • Built-in pop filter and shock mount included — 90% of the SM7B sound with none of the gain headaches
Cons
  • $299 — still a premium USB mic when the Wave:3 costs $150
  • Doesn't fully match the SM7B's depth for broadcast purists
  • Auto level and DSP are USB-only — over XLR it behaves like a plain dynamic
  • The 50 Hz–16 kHz response tops out lower than the SM7B's 50 Hz–20 kHz — less top-end air
  • No 32-bit float on the USB path — a loud take clips, where the NT1's float format recovers it
Elgato Wave:3
Pros
  • The best-sounding USB condenser for streamers who want a clean, detailed voice
  • Clipguard circuitry prevents clipping when you get excited
  • Capacitive mute, multi-function dial, and a built-in multi-layer pop filter

  • USB only — no XLR path to grow into an interface setup
  • Condenser hears the room — best in a treated or quiet space
  • Its mixing superpower depends on Wave Link software
  • The 70 Hz–20 kHz response rolls off deep lows earlier than the SM7B's 50 Hz — kick and bass sound thinner
  • At $150 it's $149 below the MV7+, but you give up XLR, DSP, and zero-latency monitoring

  • As a condenser it needs 48V phantom power on the XLR path — a bare USB-C port won't drive it

  • The fixed 80 Hz low-cut is the only onboard tone control

USB Mic vs XLR: Which Workflow Do You Need?

USB and XLR are both legitimate paths for streaming — the right one depends on your room and your expansion plans. The difference is not sound quality at this level; it is the path your signal takes and how far that path can grow.

USB mics win on simplicity. Plug in, select the device, and stream. No interface, no phantom power, no mixer. For streamers, podcasters, and video calls on a laptop, that one-cable setup saves real time and removes whole classes of failure. Modern USB mics like the MV7+ and Wave:3 include their own DSP and zero-latency monitoring.

XLR separates your mic from your converter. A dedicated interface upgrades your preamps and ADC independently of the capsule. Swap the mic without changing the interface, or upgrade the interface while keeping a great microphone. USB locks the two together.

Hybrid mics give you both. The Shure MV7+ and the Rode NT1 5th Generation connect via USB-C and XLR, so you can start on USB today and move to an interface tomorrow without buying a new microphone.

Choose by your expansion ceiling. If you will only ever stream your own voice from one desk, a great USB condenser or hybrid mic is genuinely enough. If a second mic, an instrument, or hardware upgrades are anywhere in your future, start with XLR now and avoid buying twice.

Is the Shure SM7B the Best Microphone for Streaming?

The SM7B is the default streamer microphone — the warm, intimate sound you hear on every major podcast and stream. Its dynamic cardioid capsule rejects room noise, keyboard clicks, and PC fan whine beautifully, which makes it perfect for untreated rooms where the acoustics are working against you. The rolled-off highs tame sibilance, and the built-in shock mount kills mechanical noise from your desk. The one catch is gain: the SM7B is a quiet mic, so pair it with a clean interface (like the Scarlett 2i2 or Apollo Twin X Gen 2) or a Cloudlifter to keep your stream noise-free. That extra gear is exactly why the hybrid USB mics below are worth a look if you want the same family sound with a single cable.

Is the Electro-Voice RE20 the Best Broadcast Mic for Streaming?

The EV RE20 is the radio broadcast standard — the flat, authoritative voice that streamers with treated rooms and XLR setups love because there is no proximity boom. Its Variable-D design kills the proximity effect, so the low end stays consistent whether you lean in at 2 inches or sit back at 6. That makes it ideal for streamers who move around while they talk. The RE20's response is smooth and natural with a subtle presence lift, which means it needs far less EQ than almost any other dynamic. It is equally legendary on kick drum, guitar cabs, and horns, making it one of the most versatile mics you can own. The tradeoffs: it is XLR only, heavier than the SM7B, and it wants a clean, high-gain preamp or Cloudlifter. If your setup is already XLR and you want the authoritative broadcast sound that defined radio, the RE20 is the pick.

Is the Shure MV7+ the Best Hybrid USB-C & XLR Microphone for Streaming?

The Shure MV7+ is the streamer-friendly version of the SM7B: the same warm Shure voice over USB-C, XLR, or both, with built-in DSP. It is a dynamic microphone with the familiar SM7B family tone, but it adds a headphone output with zero-latency monitoring, a touch screen for quick level checks, and real-time auto level mode that rides your gain while you talk. Its hybrid connectivity means you can plug it straight into your PC today and switch to an interface or mixer later without buying a new mic. The built-in pop filter and shock mount are included. For most streamers, the MV7+ gets you 90% of the SM7B sound with none of the gain headaches and half the cables.

Is the Elgato Wave:3 the Best USB Condenser Microphone for Streaming?

The Elgato Wave:3 is the best-sounding USB condenser for streamers who want a clean, detailed voice without touching an interface. Its premium condenser capsule captures more air and nuance than a dynamic, and the built-in multi-layer pop filter keeps plosives under control. Clipguard circuitry prevents clipping when you get excited, the capacitive mute is silent, and the multi-function dial controls gain, headphone volume, and mic-to-PC mix. The real superpower is Wave Link software: free mixing software that routes your mic, music, game audio, and Discord into separate channels — with per-source VST effects. If your room is treated or quiet and you want that detailed condenser sound with zero extra gear, the Wave:3 is the pick.

Is the Audio-Technica AT2040USB the Best Budget Broadcast-Style USB Mic for Streaming?

At $159, the AT2040USB brings true broadcast dynamics to a USB mic — a hypercardioid dynamic capsule that rejects room noise and feedback like a studio tool, with the simplest setup in this guide. It is a plug-and-play dynamic: no interface, no phantom power, just USB-C into your laptop and you are on air. The hypercardioid pattern is the key — it picks up your voice and mostly ignores the keyboard clicks, fans and echo around you, which is exactly what streaming and podcasting need. The touch-sensitive mute button glows red when you are live, the built-in headphone jack gives zero-latency monitoring, and 24-bit/96kHz capture keeps your voice clean and clear. It is the same broadcast-style approach as the Shure MV7 but at a fraction of the price, and it is the most forgiving USB dynamic in this guide for untreated rooms.

Is the Sennheiser Profile Streaming Set the Best Plug-and-Play USB Mic for Streaming?

At $199, the Profile Streaming Set is the most complete plug-and-play USB package in this guide — a cardioid condenser with real hardware controls and a boom arm that hides its own cable. Sennheiser skipped the software rabbit hole entirely: gain, mix and headphone volume are physical knobs on the body, mute is a soft-touch button with an LED ring, and the 3.5 mm headphone output gives you zero-latency monitoring. The 3-point self-locking boom arm routes the USB-C cable through its channel, so the whole setup looks clean on camera, and a 3 m cable gives you room to place it exactly where your voice sounds best. It's USB-C powered on Mac, iPad, Windows and Android with no drivers, and the all-metal housing feels built to last. The trade-off vs the Wave:3: no mixing software for game audio and mic in one stream, so think about whether you want hardware controls or software routing. If you want the cleanest desk and the most direct controls, the Profile Streaming Set is the best USB mic you can buy at $199.

Is the Rode NT1 5th Generation the Best Microphone for Streaming?

If your room has some acoustic treatment and you want the richest, most detailed voice on stream — for singing, ASMR, or professional voiceover — the NT1 5th Generation is the move. It is a studio condenser with an ultra-low 4dBA self-noise and 32-bit float, so it captures more nuance, air, and transient detail than any dynamic microphone can. Conclusion

The best stream mic is the one that fits your room and your setup today. If you want the iconic broadcast sound and already have an interface, get the SM7B. If you want that same warm Shure voice with a single USB cable and no gain issues, get the MV7+. If your room is quiet and you want detailed condenser sound with free mixing software, get the Wave:3. And if you want maximum vocal detail for singing or ASMR and your room is treated, the NT1 5th Generation delivers. For the flat, authoritative radio voice on an XLR rig — and the most versatile dynamic you can own — the EV RE20 is the classic choice. Every one of these seven is a professional, stream-ready microphone — start with the one that matches your workflow and upgrade later if you need to.
