Best Podcast Mic: SM58 to SM7B (Pro Test)

Choosing a microphone for podcasting depends more on your recording environment than your budget. A dynamic mic in an untreated room will always sound better than a condenser in the same space — so we've built this guide as a four-part journey: budget dynamics, the broadcast upgrade, the industry standard, and the high-detail condenser option.

By Daniel Carnago · August 2026

Touring and session musician with 20+ years on stages around the world. A great deal of the gear I recommend here I have used myself, on tours, in professional studios, or in my own studio.

How I test gear: to be honest, I have not tested every product on this site. Much of it I have used myself in live shows and recording sessions; others I have seen tested by musicians and sound techs I trust; and the rest I have researched in depth before recommending.
Verdict
For most shows, a $99 dynamic (SM58 or PodMic) with a solid interface is the honest sweet spot at ~$300. The Procaster ($229) adds full broadcast timbre if your interface has strong preamps. The SM7B + Cloudlifter remains the professional standard, and the NT1 5th Gen is the high-detail pick for treated rooms.
Shure SM58
Pros
  • At $99 it’s the best entry point — with an interface it totals around $300
  • Slight presence boost helps the voice come through clearly
  • Built-in pop filter and rugged build need no extra accessories
  • Buying several SM58s is far more economical than outfitting everyone with SM7Bs
Cons
  • Its spherical pop filter adds a slight warm coloration
  • Still needs an interface or mixer for its XLR connection
  • The SM7B and Procaster resolve more detail at the top end
  • Its 50 Hz–15 kHz response rolls off the top end sooner than broadcast mics
  • The $99 price doesn’t include a shock mount — desk noise transfers through a clamp
Rode PodMic
Pros
  • Built specifically for podcasting — internal pop filter and integrated swing mount
  • Warm, broadcast-tuned tone with a slightly richer low end than the SM58
  • The included mount saves buying hardware separately
  • Proven value at $99 — 4.7/5 from 17,000+ reviews
Cons
  • Gain-hungry — wants a clean 60dB from the interface
  • At 937g it’s heavy — needs a properly rated boom arm
  • Still needs an interface for its XLR connection
  • Slight proximity effect can get boomy if you work too close
Rode Procaster
Pros
  • Full-size broadcast timbre — a real step up from the SM58/PodMic
  • Tight cardioid rejects room noise, forgiving in untreated spaces
  • Internal pop filter and shock mounting built in
  • 10-year warranty and all-metal build
Cons
  • Gain-hungry — wants 60dB+ of clean gain or it hisses
  • Heavy and long — needs a solid boom arm (none included)
  • No stand or shock mount included in the box
  • 75 Hz–18 kHz response is speech-tuned, not for instruments
Shure SM7B
Pros
  • Warm, flat frequency response with a gentle presence boost for natural broadcast voice
  • Built-in pop filter and shock mount save on accessories
  • Off-axis rejection that keeps untreated home rooms out of the recording
  • The mic you see on most professional podcasts
Cons
  • Needs around 60–70dB of clean gain — a Cloudlifter ($149) is basically required
  • The full rig (SM7B + Cloudlifter + interface) runs about $750
  • It’s heavy and needs a properly rated boom arm rather than a cheap clamp
  • No USB output — the NT1 and hybrid mics record straight to a laptop without an interface
  • Its 50 Hz–20 kHz response is narrower than the NT1’s 20 Hz–20 kHz
Rode NT1 5th Generation
Pros
  • 4 dBA self-noise — lower than any dynamic microphone
  • Captures a level of detail dynamics can’t match in its price range
  • Includes a shock mount and pop filter that cost extra elsewhere
  • Dual XLR/USB output works without an interface, often on sale for $229
Cons
  • Your room must be acoustically treated — it captures every reverb, fan, and hum
  • In an untreated room the result is hollow and amateurish
  • As a condenser it needs 48V phantom power from the interface
  • 32-bit float only pays off in software that supports it — some DAWs still cap at 24-bit
  • At $269 it’s $170 above the SM58, and its extra detail is wasted until the room is treated

Is the Shure SM7B the Best Dynamic vs Condenser for Recording?

The main difference between a dynamic mic and a condenser is sensitivity. Condensers capture more detail: the air around the voice, subtle nuances, full frequency response. They also capture more background noise: the computer fan, street traffic, room reverb.

Dynamic microphones are less sensitive. Their cardioid pattern focuses on what's directly in front of them and rejects ambient sound. In a space without acoustic treatment — most home rooms — a dynamic mic delivers a more usable recording with less editing.

The practical recommendation: start with a dynamic mic. If your room has acoustic treatment (panels, bass traps, carpets), a condenser can give more detail and a more open sound. If not, the dynamic mic will save you post-production headaches. For a full breakdown of microphone types, see our best microphone guide.

Part 1: The Budget Workhorses — SM58 & PodMic

If your podcast is just getting started, the smartest money is a $99 dynamic. The Shure SM58 is the most used live vocal mic on earth, and the Rode PodMic is built specifically for podcasting — an internal pop filter, an integrated swing mount and a warm, broadcast-tuned tone. Either one records clean, intelligible speech in an untreated room, which is exactly the situation most new shows face.

The real difference is the mic’s character. The SM58 has a subtle presence lift that cuts through, plus a rugged build that survives being dropped into a gig bag for years. The PodMic sounds slightly richer and more “radio” in the lows, and it ships with the mounting hardware you’d otherwise buy separately. Both need an XLR interface and gain that doesn’t choke — the PodMic especially wants a clean 60dB or so.

When to buy. If the show is a hobby or you’re outfitting a co-host cheaply, either one. Pick the SM58 for proven road toughness and resale; pick the PodMic if you want that slightly bigger broadcast voicing and the included mount.

Part 2: The Broadcast Upgrade — Rode Procaster

The Procaster is the answer when the SM58 or PodMic has you sounding “home-recorded” and you want the full radio timbre without paying SM7B money. At $229 it’s the classic “step up” broadcast mic — a full-size dynamic with a tight cardioid pattern, a tailored voice response from 75Hz up, and internal pop filtering and shock mounting that keep plosives and desk rumble out of the take.

Where it beats the budget pair. The larger capsule and focused voicing give your voice more body and presence, and the difference is obvious the first time you compare A/B. It also rejects room noise like a champion, so it forgives an untreated space that would flatter a condenser.

The honest catch. The Procaster is gain-hungry — it wants 60dB or more of clean gain, so a Scarlett 2i2 or any interface with weak preamps will hiss at you. Pair it with something like the best audio interface recommendations (strong preamps) or add an inline booster, and budget for a boom arm because it’s heavy and nothing is included to hang it from.

Part 3: The Industry Standard — Shure SM7B

The SM7B is the microphone you see on most professional podcasts — and for good reason. Its frequency response is warm and flat, with a gentle presence boost that makes the voice sound natural without being harsh. It includes a built-in pop filter and shock mount, and its off-axis rejection is excellent in untreated rooms.

The cost of the gold standard. The mic itself is $399, but the real bill comes with the gain. The SM7B wants around 60–70dB of clean gain, so most interfaces need a Cloudlifter (≈$149) in front of it — putting the full rig near $750. It’s also heavy and needs a properly rated boom arm.

Is it worth it? If your room is treated and your budget allows, yes — it’s the safest, most classic broadcast voice you can buy, and it holds resale value. If you’re on a tighter budget, the Procaster delivers much of the same character for almost half the money. See our SM7B vs NT1 comparison for the condenser alternative.

Part 4: The High-Detail Option — Rode NT1 5th Generation

The NT1 5th Generation is the choice when your room is treated and you want a level of detail dynamics can’t match. Its self-noise is only 4dBA — lower than any dynamic — and it captures the air and nuance around the voice. It includes a shock mount and pop filter that cost extra with other mics, and its dual XLR/USB output means it records straight into a laptop with no interface at all.

The warning applies to every condenser. In an untreated room it captures every reverb, fan and hum, and the result sounds hollow and amateurish. As a condenser it also needs 48V phantom power from the interface, and its 32-bit float mode only pays off in software that supports it.

Who should pick it. Solo hosts in a treated space who want the most natural, detailed voice — and who like the idea of skipping the interface via USB. Everyone else should stay with a dynamic until the room is under control. For more, read the SM7B vs NT1 deep dive.

Do You Need an Interface?

Every XLR mic here needs an interface to reach your computer. The Scarlett 2i2 is the classic budget partner and any of the best audio interfaces will drive the SM58, PodMic and NT1 easily. If you run a two-mic show, see our streaming interfaces roundup for options with two XLR preamps and hardware mixing — or check the dedicated Rodecaster Pro II vs DLZ Creator showdown if you want an all-in-one production console.

Conclusion

For most podcasters, the honest answer is a $99 dynamic (SM58 or PodMic) with a solid interface — around $300 total, and it delivers professional sound from episode one. If you want the full radio timbre without SM7B money, the Procaster ($229) is the sweet spot, as long as your interface has strong preamps. If your budget is bigger and your room is treated, the SM7B with Cloudlifter is the industry standard — and the NT1 5th Generation offers exceptional detail for $269 in a treated room, with the bonus of USB recording. The most important decision isn't the mic — it's your recording environment. For more on microphone selection, see our best microphone guide.
