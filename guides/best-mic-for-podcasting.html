Best Microphone for Podcasting (2026) — Top Picks for Voice Recording

By Daniel Carnago · Jul 2026

Choosing a microphone for podcasting depends more on your recording environment than your budget.

Is the Shure SM7B the Best Dynamic vs Condenser for Recording?

Buy the Shure SM7B if you need a professional microphone. The main difference between a dynamic mic and a condenser is sensitivity. Condensers capture more detail: the air around the voice, subtle nuances, full frequency response. They also capture more background noise: the computer fan, street traffic, room reverb.

Dynamic microphones are less sensitive. Their cardioid pattern focuses on what's directly in front of them and rejects ambient sound. In a space without acoustic treatment — most home rooms — a dynamic mic delivers a more usable recording with less editing.

The practical recommendation: start with a dynamic mic. If your room has acoustic treatment (panels, bass traps, carpets), a condenser can give more detail and a more open sound. If not, the dynamic mic will save you post-production headaches. For a full breakdown of microphone types, see our best microphone guide.

Is the Shure SM7B the Best Shure SM7B for Recording?

Buy the Shure SM7B if you need a professional microphone. The Shure SM7B is the microphone you see on most professional podcasts. Its frequency response is warm and flat, with a gentle presence boost that makes the voice sound natural without being harsh. It includes a built-in pop filter and shock mount, and its off-axis rejection is excellent.

The SM7B's critical issue is its low output. It needs a lot of gain: around 60-70 dB to reach optimal recording levels. Most interfaces under $500 can't deliver that gain without adding noise. The standard fix is a Cloudlifter CL-1 or FetHead, which add 20-25 dB of clean gain before the interface preamp. Without one of these, the SM7B can sound noisy on entry-level interfaces.

The total cost of an SM7B ($399) plus a Cloudlifter ($149) plus an interface like the Scarlett 2i2 ($199) comes to around $750. It's a significant investment, but it's a setup that will last for years. If your budget allows, this combination is hard to beat for podcasting. For a comparison with other top podcast mics, see our SM7B vs NT1 comparison.

Is the Shure SM58 the Best Shure SM58 for Recording?

Buy the Shure SM58 if you need a professional microphone. The Shure SM58 costs $99 and is the most used live vocal mic in the world. For podcasting, it delivers perfectly acceptable quality: its frequency response has a slight presence boost that helps the voice come through clearly. In a direct comparison, the difference from an SM7B is subtle, especially after compression and EQ in the mix.

The SM58 is rugged and needs no extra accessories. It comes with a built-in pop filter and doesn't require a special shock mount. For multi-person podcasts, buying several SM58s is far more economical than outfitting everyone with an SM7B.

The SM58 has one quirk some people mod: the spherical pop filter adds a slight warm coloration. If you prefer a more open sound, you can swap it for the flat grille of an SM57, which lets more highs through.

Is the Rode NT1-A the Best Rode NT1-A for Recording?

Buy the Rode NT1-A if you need a professional microphone. The Rode NT1-A has a self-noise of only 4.5 dBA, lower than any dynamic microphone. It captures the voice with a level of detail that dynamics can't match in its price range. It includes a shock mount and pop filter — accessories that cost extra with other mics.

The catch is that your room must be acoustically treated. The NT1-A captures everything: the voice, but also the reverb off the walls, traffic noise, the refrigerator hum. In a room with acoustic panels, carpets, and bass treatment, the result is a clarity that no dynamic can deliver. In an untreated room, the result is hollow and amateurish.

The price is around $159, often on sale for $129. It's an excellent value for anyone with the right space. For those who don't have it, the SM58 will give a better immediate result.

Is the Focusrite Scarlett 2i2 4th Gen the Best USB vs XLR for Recording?

Buy the Focusrite Scarlett 2i2 4th Gen if you need a professional microphone. USB microphones are convenient: they work without an interface, connect directly to your computer, and are more portable. For a solo podcast with no plans to expand, a USB mic like the Audio-Technica ATR2100x-USB does the job well.

The advantage of an XLR system is modularity. An interface like the Scarlett 2i2 ($199) with an SM58 ($99) costs $298. If you later want to add a co-host, you buy another SM58 and the interface already has two inputs. If you want to upgrade the mic, you buy an SM7B and keep the interface. With USB, any change means replacing everything.

The upfront cost difference isn't huge: a good USB mic runs between $130 and $200. An entry-level XLR setup costs around $300. For the difference, you get better sound quality (dedicated interface preamps sound better than USB mic preamps), room to expand, and higher resale value. For anyone planning to take podcasting seriously, XLR is the better investment.

Verdict SM58 + Scarlett 2i2 is the best entry point at ~$300. SM7B + Cloudlifter is the professional standard.
