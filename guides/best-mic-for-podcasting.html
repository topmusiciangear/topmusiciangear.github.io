Best Podcast Microphones: Complete Guide (2026)

A podcast microphone needs to capture clear, detailed voice without picking up room noise. Dynamic mics reject background noise better than condensers, making them ideal for untreated rooms. Condenser mics capture more detail and nuance, making them better for treated studios. This guide covers the best podcast microphones at every price point.

By Daniel Carnago · August 2026

Touring and session musician with 20+ years on stages around the world. A great deal of the gear I recommend here I have used myself, on tours, in professional studios, or in my own studio.

Verdict
Shure SM7B for premium podcasting, Audio-Technica AT2005USB for budget, Rode NT1 for treated studios, Rode PodMic for multi-host shows.
Shure SM58
Pros
  • it’s the best entry point — with an interface it totals
  • Slight presence boost helps the voice come through clearly
  • Built-in pop filter and rugged build need no extra accessories
  • Buying several SM58s is far more economical than outfitting everyone with SM7Bs
Cons
  • Its spherical pop filter adds a slight warm coloration
  • Still needs an interface or mixer for its XLR connection
  • The SM7B and Procaster resolve more detail at the top end
  • Its 50 Hz–15 kHz response rolls off the top end sooner than broadcast mics
  • The price doesn’t include a shock mount — desk noise transfers through a clamp
Rode PodMic
Pros
  • Built specifically for podcasting — internal pop filter and integrated swing mount
  • Warm, broadcast-tuned tone with a slightly richer low end than the SM58
  • The included mount saves buying hardware separately
  • Proven value — 4.7/5 from 17,000+ reviews
Cons
  • Gain-hungry — wants a clean 60dB from the interface
  • At 937g it’s heavy — needs a properly rated boom arm
  • Still needs an interface for its XLR connection
  • Slight proximity effect can get boomy if you work too close
Rode Procaster
Pros
  • Full-size broadcast timbre — a real step up from the SM58/PodMic
  • Tight cardioid rejects room noise, forgiving in untreated spaces
  • Internal pop filter and shock mounting built in
  • 10-year warranty and all-metal build
Cons
  • Gain-hungry — wants 60dB+ of clean gain or it hisses
  • Heavy and long — needs a solid boom arm (none included)
  • No stand or shock mount included in the box
  • 75 Hz–18 kHz response is speech-tuned, not for instruments
Shure SM7B
Pros
  • Warm, flat frequency response with a gentle presence boost for natural broadcast voice
  • Built-in pop filter and shock mount save on accessories
  • Off-axis rejection that keeps untreated home rooms out of the recording
  • The mic you see on most professional podcasts
Cons
  • Needs around 60–70dB of clean gain — a Cloudlifter is basically required
  • The full rig (SM7B + Cloudlifter + interface) runs
  • It’s heavy and needs a properly rated boom arm rather than a cheap clamp
  • No USB output — the NT1 and hybrid mics record straight to a laptop without an interface
  • Its 50 Hz–20 kHz response is narrower than the NT1’s 20 Hz–20 kHz
Rode NT1 5th Generation
Pros
  • 4 dBA self-noise — lower than any dynamic microphone
  • Captures a level of detail dynamics can’t match in its price range
  • Includes a shock mount and pop filter that cost extra elsewhere
  • Dual XLR/USB output works without an interface, often on sale
Cons
  • Your room must be acoustically treated — it captures every reverb, fan, and hum
  • In an untreated room the result is hollow and amateurish
  • As a condenser it needs 48V phantom power from the interface
  • 32-bit float only pays off in software that supports it — some DAWs still cap at 24-bit
  • it’s above the SM58, and its extra detail is wasted until the room is treated

Dynamic vs Condenser for Podcasts

Dynamic microphones are the safe choice for most podcasters. The Shure SM7B ($400) is the most famous podcast microphone in the world — it's been used on countless shows including The Joe Rogan Experience. The Audio-Technica AT2005USB ($80) is the budget equivalent with both USB and XLR outputs.

Condenser microphones like the Rode NT1 ($230) capture more detail and high-frequency information, making voices sound richer and more present. But they also pick up more room noise, keyboard clicks, and computer fans. If your room isn't acoustically treated, a condenser will make your podcast sound worse, not better.

Multi-Host Podcast Setup

Multi-host podcasts need matching microphones and a multi-channel interface. Each host should use the same microphone model for consistent sound. The Rode PodMic ($100) and Shure MV7+ ($270) are popular choices for multi-host setups because they reject bleed from other hosts' microphones.

Conclusion

For untreated rooms, get a dynamic mic (Shure SM7B for premium, AT2005USB for budget). For treated studios, a condenser (Rode NT1) captures more detail. For multi-host podcasts, use matching mics (Rode PodMic) with a multi-channel interface (multi-channel interface).
