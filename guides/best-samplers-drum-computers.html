Best Samplers & Beat Making: Complete Guide

A sampler turns any sound into an instrument; a drum computer makes rhythm from scratch. From building beats on tour to designing sounds in the studio, the machines here are what modern production runs on.

By Daniel Carnago · August 2026

Touring and session musician with 20+ years on stages around the world. A great deal of the gear I recommend here I have used myself, on tours, in professional studios, or in my own studio.

Verdict
The MPC Live III and MPC Key 37 Gen 2 are the heavy-hitting all-in-one workstations for modern hip-hop, while the Digitakt II rules deep electronic sound design. Pocket picks (EP-133, P-6), live tools (Circuit Rhythm, SP-404MKII) and flagships (MPC XL, Maschine+) cover the rest — choose by workflow, not brand.
Akai MPC One G2
Pros
  • Full standalone MPC engine, no computer needed
  • 64 GB storage, WiFi, Bluetooth and touchscreen
  • Sixteen velocity pads plus plugin synths
  • Best price into real standalone production
Cons
  • USB-C allows up to 24 channels of bidirectional audio (24 in / 24 out) to a computer or compatible interface, so routing is no longer severely limited in the studio
  • Audible fan noise in quiet rooms
  • Deep menus slow quick edits
  • Pads demand firm hits for full velocity
Elektron Digitakt II
Pros
  • Eight stereo sampling tracks with Overbridge
  • Parameter locks and trig conditions for living patterns
  • 20 GB internal plus streaming for big libraries
  • The best hardware step sequencer in its class
Cons
  • No touchscreen, encoders and buttons only
  • Overbridge via USB provides individual track outputs directly to the computer in digital format, so the physical output limitation can be bypassed with the official software
  • Steep learning curve for Elektron newcomers
  • Overbridge stutters on crowded USB hubs
Akai MPC Live III
Pros
  • Sixteen audio tracks in a portable box
  • Built-in lithium battery plus monitoring speaker
  • Seven-inch touchscreen with legendary MPC pads
  • Standalone and controller modes in one unit
Cons
  • Heavy 2.2 kg chassis, no pocket box
  • 6 physical line outputs (3 stereo pairs) inherited from the Live II, plus up to 24 independent audio channels via USB-C
  • Premium price for the portable privilege
  • Built-in speaker rattles on sub-bass notes
Roland SP-404MKII
Pros
  • Sixteen sample tracks with 12 effects per pattern
  • Skip-back sampling catches happy accidents
  • Legendary DJ FX looper for live sets
  • The definitive lo-fi and boom-bap workflow
Cons
  • No serious MIDI sequencing to speak of
  • Small screen complicates waveform edits
  • No song mode for full arrangements
  • Resampling sessions eat SD cards fast
Native Instruments Maschine+
Pros
  • Standalone groovebox and software controller in one
  • 16 GB factory library with WiFi and Bluetooth
  • Eight groups of sixteen hyper-responsive pads
  • Lock snapshots for instant arrangement shifts
Cons
  • High price for the standalone privilege
  • Fan noise bleeds into quiet recordings
  • Locked inside the NI software ecosystem
  • Slow boot-up kills spontaneous ideas
Teenage Engineering EP-133 K.O. II
Pros
  • Pocket-size sampler with vintage calculator charm
  • Punch-in 2.0 effects for live performance flair
  • Built-in microphone plus pressure-sensitive keys
  • The most fun per dollar in sampling
Cons
  • 128 MB of memory caps longer phrases
  • No real resampling workflow inside
  • Delicate plastic build needs careful hands
  • No proper song mode for full arrangements
Akai MPC Key 37 Gen 2
Pros
  • Complete MPC engine with 37 full-size keys
  • Aftertouch keyboard for expressive playing
  • Massive I/O including multi-voltage CV/Gate
  • Pads and keys finally share one standalone box
Cons
  • Much larger footprint than desktop MPCs
  • Seven-inch screen feels small beside the keys
  • Extra keyboard weight complicates travel
  • MPC 3 OS adds the Linear Arranger (DAW-style linear sequencer), solving the old menu-driven workflow issues for song structuring with a physical keyboard
Roland Aira Compact P-6
Pros
  • Pocket-size sampler with real granular engine
  • USB-C audio and MIDI sampling from phones
  • Famous Roland SP master effects on board
  • Rechargeable battery for beats on the move
Cons
  • Tiny cryptic screen means learning shortcuts
  • Limited polyphony for dense arrangements
  • Mini pads fight serious finger-drumming
  • Micro chassis feels more toy than tool
Novation Circuit Rhythm
Pros
  • Screenless grid workflow keeps ears in charge
  • Eight sample tracks with hands-on Grid FX
  • Rechargeable battery with four hours of play
  • Seamless pattern switching for live sets
Cons
  • No waveform view without memorizing combos
  • Sample backup depends on the Components app
  • Limited memory per project caps long sets
  • Eight monophonic tracks restrict layering
Akai MPC XL
Pros
  • Flagship studio center with 10.1-inch touchscreen
  • Sixteen motorized Q-Link knobs with OLED screens
  • 16 CV/Gate outputs across 8 TRS connectors
  • The largest pads and best ergonomics in MPC land
Cons
  • Flagship price, the dearest box here
  • Desk-filling footprint, zero portability
  • The massive chassis dissipates heat passively and efficiently, with no fan noise interfering with close studio microphones
  • Overkill for producers making simple loops

How to Choose the Best Sampler for Your Workflow?

Standalone or tethered, pads or keys, screen or no screen — the right sampler disappears into your habits. If you finish tracks on the couch or on tour, pick a standalone box with battery and storage. If you live inside a DAW, a controller-style workflow with deep software integration will take you further.

Budget draws the other line: pocket samplers sketch ideas for under $300, mid-range workstations cover full productions, and flagships replace the studio desk. The ten machines below cover every one of those lanes.

Hands-On Value: The MPC One G2 Way

The cheapest door into the standalone MPC world, with nothing essential left out. The One G2 runs the full MPC software engine — 16 velocity pads, plugin synths, touchscreen, WiFi and Bluetooth — so beats start and finish without a computer in sight.

A single stereo output and some menu diving remind you where the price went, and the pads ask for firm hits. For hip-hop and urban producers entering standalone territory, no box gives more per dollar.

Conclusion

Choose your machine by workflow and habits, not by hype: if you want to make quick sketches anywhere, your place is with the EP-133 K.O. II or the Roland P-6; if live transitions and effects are your thing, your flow will fit Circuit Rhythm or the SP-404MKII; for massive productions and full-song arrangement, you need the Maschine+ ecosystem or the MPC range. The Digitakt II will reward analytical minds who love step sequencing, the Key 37 Gen 2 will give wings to those who think through keys, and the imposing MPC XL will be the definitive centerpiece for studios that want to rule everything from a single hardware throne.
